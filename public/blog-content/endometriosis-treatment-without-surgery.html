What Is Endometriosis?

This condition affects women in lots of different ways. Understanding the basics will empower you to figure out who to turn to for support.

What Does Endometriosis Mean and What’s The Overview?

Endometriosis is a condition where some of the tissue in your uterus starts growing outside of it, often on your ovaries, fallopian tubes or the lining of your pelvis. And just like the tissue inside your uterus, this misplaced tissue gets all inflamed and bleeds every month, but then it has no way out. That causes scarring and adhesions, which can cause all sorts of problems.

How Common Is Endometriosis in Women?

Like 10% of women of reproductive age have it worldwide. Symptoms usually become most noticeable between 25-35 years old, but often stay hidden for many years.

How Does Endometriosis Affect the Reproductive System?

It can cause all sorts of problems like inflamed tissues, blocked tubes, or even problems with egg quality. But still, a lot of women manage to get pregnant with some help. And here’s the thing, each woman’s symptoms are going to be totally different. Some will be super obvious, while others will be completely hidden. So let’s take a look at what to keep an eye out for.

What Are The Common Endometriosis Symptoms

Your symptoms or the lack of them are totally valid. Every woman’s experience with endometriosis is unique.

  • Pain-Related Symptoms to Watch Out For

    • Pelvic pain that worsens during periods
    • Discomfort during sex, bowel movements, or urination
    • Fatigue, bloating, and nausea
    • The thing is, with endometriosis, the amount of pain you feel doesn’t necessarily match how bad the disease actually is. You can have really bad pain with barely any symptoms at all, or lots of symptoms with hardly any pain.

  • Menstrual and Pelvic Changes

    • Really heavy or irregular bleeding
    • Spotting in between periods
    • Persistent tenderness in your pelvis

  • Signs That Might Affect Fertility

    • Infertility impacts up to 50% of women with endometriosis
    • Scarring or inflammation plays a role
    • Many still conceive with support
Some women won’t have any symptoms at all, that’s called silent or asymptomatic endometriosis. In fact, it’s estimated that 20-25% of cases are completely symptom-free. You might only find out you have it when you go for a fertility check. Whatever your case, your body still deserves attention. All this variation makes us look at the possible causes of endometriosis and how they might all play a part.

What Causes Endometriosis?

We can’t find the exact endometriosis causes. But we do think that several factors might be involved.

  • Medical and Hormonal Factors That Might Play A Role

    • Retrograde menstruation (backward flow of menstrual blood)
    • Estrogen fueling tissue growth
    • Immune system not clearing misplaced cells

  • Factors That Might Make You More Likely To Get It

    • Family history
    • Early periods, short cycles, or heavy bleeding
    • Never having given birth

  • How Endometriosis Progresses Over Time

    • It can creep up on you and get worse, creating more and more damage as it goes. Some women find that pregnancy brings a temporary reprieve from the worst of the symptoms. Knowing what causes it helps doctors pick the best treatment for you. Surgery is one tool that’s often used, but it’s not always the answer.

Is Endometriosis Surgery Always Necessary?

A lot of women worry that surgery is the only way to fix things. But for most of them, it isn’t.

  • When Doctors Decide Surgery is the Way to Go

    • Severe, unresponsive pain
    • Large cysts or organ blockages
    • Laparoscopy to diagnose and remove tissue

  • Limitations and Risks of Surgical Treatment

    • Pain relief may be short-term
    • Recurrence is possible
    • Risks include infection or new adhesions

  • Cases Where Surgery May Not Be Required

    • Mild to moderate symptoms that respond to other methods
    • Well-managed cases without complications

Non-Surgical Treatment Options for Endometriosis

Endometriosis treatment often starts out gently and can be pretty effective from the get-go.

  • Medications and Hormonal Therapy

    • These options can cut your pain, control inflammation, and even help slow down the growth of the endometrium by reducing estrogen levels.
    • NSAIDs to help with pain relief
    • The pill or progestins to suppress estrogen
    • GnRH agonists for more control over the condition
    • Be sure to seek advice from your gynecologist or fertility specialist before doing anything.

  • Lifestyle Changes That Can Help with Endometriosis

    • Regular, gentle exercise to keep things moving
    • Eating an anti-inflammatory diet
    • Stress reduction, pelvic floor therapy, or trying out acupuncture
